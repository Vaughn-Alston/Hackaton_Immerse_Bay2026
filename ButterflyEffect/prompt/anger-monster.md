Create a cinematic, subjective-third-person interactive story about an anger monster that follows people through everyday situations. At every choice, the audience must immediately understand that they are choosing for the person currently carrying the monster.

**CAMERA — VERY IMPORTANT**

Never use literal first-person perspective. At a choice, briefly establish the carrier's tense face, then settle just behind the carrier's left shoulder: their cheek and shoulder dominate the near foreground, the monster is unmistakably visible beside their neck, and the other person's face is centered in their eyeline. This is the audience's person. Never place the camera neutrally between the two people.

When a monster first forms on someone, cut to an extreme close-up and hold their silent contemplation before offering choices. Use restrained screen acting: they freeze mid-task and hold a small breath; their eyes widen only slightly, then become heavy-lidded and drop into a distant downward gaze; their brow gathers, jaw shifts once, and lips almost form a response before closing. Hold the silence, then let their gaze return deliberately to the other person. Do not add explanatory dialogue.

For driving scenes, position the camera at a three-quarter side angle, looking through the driver's side window, with enough distance to see the driver's face, upper body, steering wheel, and the monster looming behind their shoulder or in the rear passenger area. Alternate with wider exterior tracking shots. Keep both characters visually readable, with minimal window glare.

**MONSTER DESIGN**

The anger monster is a tall, hunched, shadowy humanoid with a clearly defined head, shoulders, long arms, and two small, glowing ember-red eyes. Its body is made of dense black shadow with subtle smoke drifting from its edges. It has no clothes, accessories, exaggerated teeth, or cartoon expressions.

It should be unsettling, intimidating, and almost human, like a dark presence silently watching.

The monster is invisible to the characters but visible to the audience. It mirrors their frustration and moves closer when they become angry. It grows more imposing when anger escalates.

**OPENING SCENE — ROAD RAGE**

Golden-hour traffic. A tired man drives home after a stressful day. Another driver suddenly cuts into his lane, forcing him to brake.

Show his realistic reaction: tightened jaw, furrowed brow, irritated breathing, and hands gripping the steering wheel.

The anger monster slowly rises from the shadows behind his seat. Its ember-red eyes brighten as the driver's frustration builds. It leans toward the driver as though encouraging him to react.

Hold the carrier-aligned over-shoulder composition so the audience sees the driver's anger and the monster together in the near foreground, with the other driver's reaction beyond them.

**INTERACTIVE CHOICES**

Present exactly three choices, based on believable human behavior:

A. Tailgate the other car to show frustration.

B. Honk angrily and shout at the driver.

C. Take a breath, create distance, and let the other driver go.

Do not generate random, funny, magical, or cosmetic choices. Every choice must describe a realistic human action that affects the emotional situation.

**CONTINUING STORY**

If the viewer chooses A or B, show realistic consequences. The anger monster grows stronger. If someone else becomes upset because of that behavior, transfer the monster to that person and follow their next everyday conflict.

If the viewer chooses a restrained response, the monster becomes quieter but can remain nearby.

Continue with naturally connected situations involving strangers, coworkers, family members, customers, and other everyday interactions.

Every new scene must show the person, the anger monster, and a believable reason for frustration. Provide three realistic emotional choices.

The story ends when the viewer chooses a genuinely compassionate response that interrupts the cycle. Show the monster slowly losing its shape, its red eyes fading, and its shadow dissolving completely.

**STRICT RULES**

- Subjective third-person camera aligned with the current monster-carrier; never literal first-person.
- Always show the human and monster together when possible.
- At every choice, keep the carrier large in the foreground and the other person beyond them; never use a neutral or symmetrical two-shot.
- For consequences, stay on the same side of the eyeline and rack focus from the carrier's action to the other person's reaction and the monster's transfer.
- Whenever a character responds through speech or physical action, their face and upper body must turn toward the person receiving that response, with readable eye contact. The only exception is when the chosen action is explicitly to ignore, avoid, turn away from or walk past that person; then the broken eyeline is the action.
- Keep the exact same monster appearance throughout.
- No cartoon monsters or comedic behavior.
- No random suggestions like snacks, sunglasses, or freezing cars.
- No camera angles that hide the monster.
- All choices must be realistic, consequential human responses.
- Anger itself is natural; passing it to other people strengthens the monster.
- Kindness ultimately breaks the cycle.

Realistic acting, cinematic lighting, grounded psychological horror, clear character framing, subtle expressions, and emotionally believable storytelling.
